Self-Custody: Owning Your Own Keys

What self-custody actually means, what it costs, and how to do it without making rookie mistakes. For people who hold enough crypto that leaving it on an exchange feels wrong.

Intermediate 45 minutes 5 steps

Who this is for

You hold enough crypto that leaving it all on an exchange feels wrong — but you're not sure what "self-custody" actually means, whether you need it, or how to do it without making a catastrophic mistake.

This path takes about 45 minutes. By the end you'll have a clear framework for deciding what to keep in cold storage, what to keep in a hot wallet, and how to set up recovery so a single point of failure doesn't wipe you out.

The mental model

Self-custody means you hold the private keys, not an exchange. The trade is trust: instead of trusting an exchange to liquidate correctly and not commingle your funds, you're trusting yourself to not lose a piece of paper, get phished, or forget a passphrase.

For most people, this means:

  • A hardware wallet (cold) for long-term holdings
  • A small amount in a hot wallet for active transactions
  • A disciplined seed phrase backup, ideally in two physical locations
  • A documented recovery plan that someone else can execute if you can't

When not to do this

If your holdings are small enough that you wouldn't be upset to lose them, or if you trade frequently, the costs of self-custody may exceed the benefits. The [First $100 path](/paths/first-100/) ends with a custody framework that addresses this.

Next steps

After you've set up cold storage, the [Avoiding scams path](/paths/avoiding-scams/) is the next layer of defense — it covers the social engineering and approval-based attacks that target holders with non-trivial holdings.
