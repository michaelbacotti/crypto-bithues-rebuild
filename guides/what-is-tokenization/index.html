What Is Tokenization? Real-World Assets, Digital Claims, and Real Risks

The Idea

Tokenization: represent an asset that exists in the physical world as a digital token on a blockchain.

Instead of a $10 million commercial building being owned by three private equity funds, the building might be represented by 10 million tokens, each representing $1 of ownership. Instead of a $100,000 Treasury bond being held in a brokerage account, it's a token you can hold in a crypto wallet.

The pitch is compelling: 24/7 markets, fractional ownership, instant settlement, programmable distributions. In theory.

What's Actually Being Tokenized in 2026

Treasuries (The Largest Category)

BlackRock's BUIDL fund and Franklin Templeton's BENJI are the largest tokenized treasury products, collectively holding over $2 billion in US Treasury bonds as of mid-2026. These are real products from regulated financial institutions, accessible to accredited investors.

On-chain treasuries offer: fast settlement, 24/7 transferability, transparent holdings via blockchain explorers.

Private Credit

Several platforms are tokenizing private loans — small business loans, real estate debt, consumer credit. Investors can buy fractions of loan portfolios that previously required large minimum investments.

Real Estate (Early Stage)

Tokenized real estate exists — fractions of commercial buildings, rental portfolios, land. Most are illiquid, with limited secondary markets. The legal structure is typically an LLC or similar vehicle, with the token representing a share in that entity.

Art and Collectibles

NFT-based fractional ownership of artwork. High-profile examples exist; actual liquidity is extremely low.

How It Works Technically

A tokenized asset involves several layers:

1. The Legal Layer: A traditional legal structure — an LLC, a special purpose vehicle (SPV), or a fund — actually holds the real-world asset. Token holders have a legal claim on the entity, not directly on the asset.

2. The Custodial Layer: A custodian holds the real-world asset and issues tokens representing beneficial ownership. This is typically a regulated entity — a trust company, a bank, or a licensed platform.

3. The Blockchain Layer: Tokens are minted on a blockchain (Ethereum, Solana, etc.) representing the legal claim. The token contract defines transferability, issuance, and redemption rules.

4. The Redemption Path: To get your money back, you redeem the token through the platform. The platform sells the underlying asset or finds a buyer, and you receive cash. This is not instant, despite the token being tradeable 24/7.

The Real Risks

Legal Ownership ≠ On-Chain Ownership

The token is a claim on a legal entity, not directly on the underlying asset. If the legal entity gets sued, or goes bankrupt, token holders are unsecured creditors. The blockchain doesn't protect you from the legal structure failing.

This is not hypothetical. In 2022, several tokenized real estate platforms had legal disputes between founders and custodians that froze investor redemptions for months.

Custodian Risk

The custodian holds the asset. If the custodian fails, goes bankrupt, or acts fraudulently, your token may represent a claim on an empty shell. Regulation of custodians varies by jurisdiction and is still catching up.

Liquidity Mismatch

Tokens are designed to be tradeable 24/7. The underlying assets — buildings, loans, bonds — are not. If everyone tries to redeem simultaneously, the platform faces a classic bank run. Most platforms have gating provisions to prevent this, meaning redemptions can be suspended.

Smart Contract Risk

The token contract on the blockchain could have bugs. In 2024, a tokenized real estate protocol lost $15M due to a smart contract vulnerability. The underlying real estate was fine; the on-chain token wrapper failed.

Regulatory Uncertainty

Are the tokens securities? In the US, the SEC has taken the position that most tokenized securities need to be registered or qualify for an exemption. Many platforms operate under specific exemptions that restrict who can buy. If you're not an accredited investor, your legal protections may be limited.

What Makes Tokenization Different From a Stock

When you buy stock in Apple, you own a small piece of Apple. The stock is the legal instrument. There's no layer beneath it.

When you buy a tokenized version of a building, you own a token that gives you a legal claim on an LLC that owns the building. The token is not the building. The LLC is. If the LLC is set up incorrectly, or if the platform goes under, your token could be worthless while the building is still worth $10 million.

This is sometimes called the "tokenization stack" — and every layer adds risk.

When Tokenization Makes Sense

Tokenization can be legitimate and useful in specific contexts:

  • Accredited investors accessing alternative investments with lower minimums than traditional private placements.
  • Treasury tokenization from regulated issuers (BlackRock BUIDL, Franklin Templeton) for cash management — if the issuer is credible and the structure is clear.
  • International investors accessing US assets with faster settlement than traditional wire transfers.

When to Be Careful

  • Any platform promising guaranteed returns or advertising tokenized assets as "like owning the real thing."
  • Platforms without clear legal structure (who holds the LLC? what's the custodian?).
  • Tokenized assets marketed to non-accredited investors with promises of high yields.
  • "DeFi-native" tokenized real estate without a regulated custodian.

The Practical Standard

Before buying any tokenized asset:

  1. Who is the legal issuer? Look up the entity.
  2. Who is the custodian?
  3. What does the token actually represent? A share of an LLC? A direct beneficial interest?
  4. What are the redemption terms? Can you exit in a day? A month? A year?
  5. Is the platform registered with a financial regulator?

If the platform can't clearly answer questions 1-3, don't invest.
